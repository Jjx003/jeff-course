# Building the Story Bank

## Step 1 — inventory

Before mapping anything to questions, list every memorable episode from the last few years. Aim for twenty raw candidates.

Prompts to shake them loose:

- A time you changed your mind about something technical.
- A time a project went sideways.
- A collaboration that was harder than expected.
- Something you built that people actually used.
- A time you were wrong in a way that mattered.
- A time you pushed back and were right. A time you pushed back and were wrong.
- A deadline you missed, or one you made by cutting something.
- Something you learned quickly under pressure.
- A time you mentored, taught, or unblocked someone.
- The most technically satisfying thing you have done.

## Step 2 — write them in STAR

**Situation, Task, Action, Result.** It is a cliché because it works: it forces you to include the context an interviewer needs and the outcome they are actually asking about.

Two rules that matter more than the acronym:

**Weight it toward Action.** A common failure is spending 90 seconds on context and 15 on what you did. Aim for roughly 20% situation and task, 60% action, 20% result. The action is the part being evaluated.

**Have a number in the result.** "It went well" is not a result. "We cut the eval turnaround from six hours to twenty minutes, and the team went from evaluating weekly to evaluating per-commit" is. A number makes the story concrete and makes you sound like someone who measures things.

## Step 3 — map stories to questions

Build a grid: stories down the side, question families across the top, and mark which stories can serve which questions.

A good story serves three or four. If you have a story that only answers one question, it is probably too narrow — or you have not found its most interesting angle.

You want at least two stories per question family, because interviewers sometimes ask for a second example and because you will use a story in an earlier interview that you wanted for a later one.

## Step 4 — rehearse out loud

Not in your head. Out loud, timed, to a wall if necessary.

Target 90 seconds to 2 minutes. Under 60 seconds and you sound like you are hiding something; over 3 minutes and you have lost them. Recording yourself once is unpleasant and worth it — almost everyone discovers they front-load context and rush the result.

# The Hard Ones

## "Tell me about a failure"

The trap is choosing something that is not really a failure ("I worked too hard and burned out") or something so catastrophic it raises new questions.

The shape that works: a real failure, with **your** contribution to it clearly owned, and a specific behavioral change that followed. The change is the point of the question. "I now write the evaluation plan before the training run, because I lost three weeks to a metric that turned out not to measure what I thought" is a complete answer.

Say "I" for the mistake. Blaming a collaborator in an interview is a strong negative signal even when the blame is accurate.

## "Tell me about a disagreement"

They are checking whether you can hold a position without damaging a relationship.

Include: what the actual technical disagreement was, how you tried to resolve it, and how it ended. **A disagreement you lost is often the better story**, because it lets you show that you can be convinced — provided you can articulate what convinced you.

Avoid: disagreements that were really personality conflicts, and any story where you are unambiguously the hero and the other person is unambiguously an idiot.

## "Tell me about difficult feedback"

They want to know whether feedback lands or bounces. The story should include feedback that was **initially hard to hear and turned out to be right**, and what you concretely changed.

The failure mode is choosing feedback you disagreed with and describing why you were right. That answers a different question.

## The AI safety or impact question

Increasingly common at labs, and it is a real question rather than a formality.

Have a considered view. What you want to convey: you have thought about it, you can hold complexity, and you are neither dismissive nor performatively anxious. Concrete beats abstract — a specific case where you thought about the consequences of something you built lands far better than a general statement about caring.

Be honest. If your view is "I take the misuse risks seriously and I am less convinced by some of the longer-term arguments", say that. Manufactured conviction is transparent and worse than disagreement.

# Questions to Ask Them

You will get 5–10 minutes at the end of most interviews. This is not a formality — it is a signal, and it is also your only real chance to gather information for a decision you may have to make.

**Good questions:**

- What does the first six months look like for someone in this role?
- What is the team working on that you are most excited about?
- How do projects get chosen here? Top-down, bottom-up, or something else?
- What is the biggest thing you would change about how the team works?
- How much of the work is research versus engineering, honestly?
- What happened to the last person who left this team?

That last one is uncomfortable and unusually informative.

**Bad questions:** anything answered on the careers page, compensation (that is the recruiter's conversation), and "what is the company culture like" — which reliably produces a non-answer.

**One thing worth doing:** ask the same question of several interviewers across the loop. Divergent answers to "how do projects get chosen" tell you something real about the organization that no single answer would.
