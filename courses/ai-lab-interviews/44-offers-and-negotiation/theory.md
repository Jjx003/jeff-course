# Before You Have an Offer

## Sequencing

The standard advice: warm up on lower-priority companies, then time the finals so offers arrive together. Roughly right, with three amendments.

**Stamina is finite.** A loop is genuinely draining. Burning six as practice and arriving exhausted at the one you care about is a real and common failure.

**Headcount beats preparation.** Whether a team is actively hiring matters more than whether you studied another week. Friends and recruiters are the only source for this — ask.

**Deadlines flex more than you think.** Recruiters know you have other processes running, and there are ordinary ways to extend a decision. Exploding offers exist but are the exception; find out which companies have that reputation before you sequence around them.

## What not to say

You will be asked for your current compensation or your expectations, often early and casually.

**Deflect the number.** "I would rather learn more about the role and the level before talking about numbers" works and is not rude. In several jurisdictions asking for salary history is illegal, and even where it is not, anchoring on your current number is strictly bad for you.

If pressed for a range, give one you would be delighted by, framed as based on your research — and be aware that a range functions as a ceiling, never a floor.

# After the Offer

## Get it in writing, in full

Base, equity, sign-on, and the details that determine what the equity is actually worth:

- **Vesting schedule** and cliff.
- **Refresh policy** — the annual grant after the initial one. Frequently omitted and often larger in aggregate than the sign-on.
- **For private companies:** strike price, current preferred price, most recent 409A, total shares outstanding. **A number of shares without the total outstanding is not information.** Ask.
- **Liquidity terms**: tender offers, secondary sales, and what happens to vested options if you leave — the exercise window matters enormously and is easy to overlook.

## Calibrate

You cannot negotiate well against an unknown market. Sources, in rough order of value:

1. **Friends who recently negotiated at that company or a peer.** By far the best data.
2. **Levels.fyi and similar**, understanding they skew high and lag the current market.
3. **The recruiter**, for band and level structure. They will often tell you what the band is even when they will not tell you where in it you sit.
4. **Your other offers.** The strongest instrument you have, and the reason timing matters.

## Prepare each conversation

Before every recruiter call, write down:

- What you are willing to share and what you are not.
- The specific ask, with a number.
- **Quotes you can say verbatim.** This sounds excessive. It is the single most useful thing in this module, because the moment is uncomfortable and improvised phrasing under discomfort is how people undersell themselves.
- Anticipated pushback, and your response to each.

## The conversation itself

**Be warm and be specific.** The recruiter is not your adversary; they usually want to close you and need a reason to go to the compensation committee. Give them one.

A shape that works:

> "I am genuinely excited about this team, and I want to make it work. The offer is currently below what I have from [other company / what my research suggests for this level]. If you could get the base to X and the initial equity to Y, I would be ready to sign."

Three things that are doing work there: enthusiasm, a specific number, and a **commitment** — "I would be ready to sign" gives the recruiter something concrete to take upstairs. Vague dissatisfaction gives them nothing.

**Ask about level, not just numbers.** Level determines the band, future raises, and scope. Moving up a level is usually worth more than moving up within one, and it is often more negotiable than people assume — particularly if a loop went well.

**Non-compensation levers** that are frequently available: start date, team placement, remote or hybrid arrangement, compute and budget commitments, conference and publication policy, sign-on to cover a forfeited bonus, relocation. For research roles, publication freedom and compute access can matter more over five years than the base salary difference.

## Silence

After you state your ask, stop talking.

The pause will feel long. Filling it with justification is the most common self-inflicted wound in salary negotiation — it reads as apologizing for the ask, and it invites the recruiter to negotiate against the weakest thing you said.

# Deciding

Compensation is the easiest dimension to compare and rarely the one that determines whether you are happy in two years.

**What tends to matter more:**

- **The team and the manager.** The single largest predictor of daily experience. If you did not meet your prospective manager, ask to.
- **What you would actually work on.** Not the team's charter — the specific projects that exist right now.
- **Compute and data access.** For research roles this is a hard constraint on what you can even attempt.
- **Publication and openness policy.** Varies enormously between labs and shapes your next five years of career optionality.
- **Trajectory.** Is the team growing? Is the work becoming more or less central to the company?
- **Whether you believe in what they are building.** Easy to dismiss and difficult to sustain a career without.

**A useful exercise:** for each offer, write the story of your first year. What are you working on in month three, month six, month twelve? The offer where that story is vague or unappealing is usually the wrong one, no matter what the number says.

**On other people's opinions.** Everyone will have one, and they will be unusually invested. Peers, advisors, family, and people on the internet all have views about where you should go, and most are optimizing for something other than your life. Gather information widely; decide narrowly.

# The Part Nobody Writes About

The emotional load is real and it is worth naming.

There is a lot of social perception to navigate. Comparing yourself to peers feels bad. Everyone has opinions. Navigating a large decision space with incomplete information, where small choices with no right answer have outsized consequences, is genuinely stressful. Months of it is exhausting, and many people find they are not functioning well in other parts of their life during it.

If that is your experience, it is not a sign you are doing it wrong. It is the normal experience of a process that is genuinely demanding, and it ends.

Two things that help concretely: a small number of people you can be honest with about how it is going, and a hard boundary on hours — the search expands to fill whatever space it is given, and past a point the extra hours do not improve outcomes.
