# Research Discussion and the Job Talk

The research discussion is the interview that most resembles the job, and the one most people prepare least for — because it feels like something you already know how to do. You have given this talk. You have answered these questions at poster sessions.

The catch is that a research interview is not a conference presentation. The interviewer is not evaluating whether the work was good; that was settled by the fact that you got the interview. They are evaluating **how you think about problems**, and specifically:

- Why did you choose *this* problem?
- What did you believe before, and what do you believe now?
- What would you do differently?
- What would you do next?

Those four questions are the interview. The paper is the excuse to ask them.

## If you do not have publications

This module still applies, and the format is friendlier than people expect. The question becomes *"tell me about the hardest technical thing you have built"*, and the same four questions follow.

What matters is that you can articulate a decision you made, the alternatives you rejected, the evidence that settled it, and what you would change. A well-told engineering story about a system you designed under real constraints beats a poorly-told paper story every time — and interviewers at labs know that most of the best engineers they have hired did not have a publication record.

## The framing that changes the interview

Interviewers are tired. They have read your CV in the ten minutes before the call. Your job in the first two minutes is to give them a **compact, correct model of what you do** that they can hold for the next hour.

That means tailoring. Not lying — tailoring. The same body of work can be honestly framed as "efficiency of language models" or "how tokenization affects downstream behavior" or "building evaluation infrastructure", and which of those you lead with should depend on the role. Hitting the keywords that make your relevance obvious is a kindness to a tired interviewer, and it is the difference between them spending the hour engaged and them spending it reorienting.

## What gets asked

- Tell me about a project you are proud of. (Expect 10–30 minutes on it.)
- Why did you work on this rather than something else?
- What was the hardest part? What surprised you?
- What is the weakness of this work?
- If you had six more months, what would you do?
- I see you also worked on X — tell me about that.
- What do you want to work on next, and why here?
