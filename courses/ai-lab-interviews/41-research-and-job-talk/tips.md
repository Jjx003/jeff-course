# The Preparation Checklist

Work through this in writing, not in your head. Written answers are shorter, sharper, and retrievable under pressure.

**For your primary project:**

- [ ] One-sentence version, comprehensible to someone outside your subfield
- [ ] Two-minute pitch: problem, why hard, key idea, evidence, implication
- [ ] Ten-minute version with the one figure you would draw on a whiteboard
- [ ] Why this problem, in a way that shows taste rather than circumstance
- [ ] What surprised you — specific, and true
- [ ] The genuine weakness, and why you shipped anyway
- [ ] Three next directions, ranked, with reasons
- [ ] The three most likely "why didn't you try X" questions, and honest answers

**For each secondary project:** the one-sentence version and one interesting detail. That is enough.

**For the role:**

- [ ] What does this team actually work on? (Read their recent papers, not their careers page.)
- [ ] Which of your work is most relevant, and how do you frame it that way honestly?
- [ ] Two specific questions about their work that show you read it
- [ ] Your answer to "why here" that is not "you are a great lab"

# Framing Without Lying

The same work, framed for three roles:

> *Efficiency team:* "I worked on why tokenizer choice changes inference cost, and how much of the cross-lingual cost gap is recoverable."

> *Pretraining team:* "I worked on how tokenizer training data interacts with model training data, and what happens when they diverge."

> *Evaluation team:* "I built the measurement infrastructure for comparing tokenizers across languages, because the existing metrics did not capture what mattered."

All three can describe one body of work. None is a lie. Which one you lead with is a choice, and making it deliberately is part of the job.

# Traps

- **Talking for ten minutes without stopping.** Pitch, then pause. Let them steer.
- **"My advisor suggested it."** Even if true, it is not an answer to "why this problem".
- **A fake weakness.** "It could be bigger" tells them you will not be honest about your own work.
- **Defending a decision you no longer believe.** "In hindsight that reasoning was weaker than I thought" is a strong sentence.
- **Inventing a control you did not run.** They will keep pulling, and it is the one genuinely fatal move.
- **Not knowing what the team works on.** It is a two-hour investment and it changes the entire conversation.
- **A chronological CV walkthrough.** Give them the through-line; do not make them derive it.

# If You Do Not Have Papers

Substitute the hardest system you have built, and answer the same four questions:

- **Why this problem?** What was actually broken, and how did you know it mattered?
- **What surprised you?** The bottleneck that was not where you expected, the assumption that turned out to be load-bearing.
- **What is wrong with it?** The design decision you would reverse, and the constraint that forced it.
- **What next?** What you would build now, and what you learned that changed your view.

One more thing worth doing: bring a **number**. "It cut p99 latency from 1.2s to 340ms" or "it reduced training cost by 40%" makes the story concrete in exactly the way a paper's headline result does. Engineering stories fail in interviews mostly for lack of a measurable outcome, not for lack of difficulty.

# Further Reading

- [Notes on the Industry Job Search](https://alisawuffles.github.io/blog/job-search/) — the research-discussion and job-talk sections in particular.
- [You and Your Research](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.html) — Hamming. Not interview advice, but the best thing ever written on what "taste" means, which is what this interview measures.
