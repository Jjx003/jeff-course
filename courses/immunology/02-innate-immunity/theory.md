# Deeper theory: thresholds, timing, and control

## A common mathematical shape

Many innate decisions can be abstracted as

$$A=\sum_i w_i s_i-\sum_j v_j h_j,$$

where $s_i$ is activating input $i$, $w_i$ is its effective weight, $h_j$ is
inhibitory input $j$, and $v_j$ is its weight. $A$ is the resulting activation
score, and action occurs when $A>\theta$, the response threshold. Receptor
abundance, prior exposure, metabolism, and tissue signals can change the inputs,
weights, or threshold. This framework fits NK
cytotoxicity, phagocyte activation, and inflammatory signaling better than a
single on/off receptor story.

## Positive feedback needs spatial brakes

Complement and cytokine circuits gain sensitivity through amplification. Safe
amplification generally requires:

1. inactive circulating precursors;
2. assembly on a target surface or signaling scaffold;
3. short component lifetimes;
4. soluble and membrane-bound inhibitors.

Removing a brake can phenocopy excessive initiation. Therefore a high output
does not tell you whether the input was too strong or regulation too weak.

## Timing can reverse interpretation

Early, local type I interferon can establish an antiviral state before viral
burden peaks. Delayed or chronic interferon can accompany tissue damage and
immune dysfunction. Plotting only a single late time point can invert the
causal story.

For a pathway measurement, always record:

- tissue or blood compartment;
- time relative to exposure and symptoms;
- cell type producing or responding;
- whether perturbation changes both pathogen burden and host damage.

## Trained immunity is not V(D)J memory

Innate cells and progenitors can be metabolically and epigenetically
reprogrammed, producing enhanced or damped later responses. This can persist,
but it does not use clonally distributed rearranged antigen receptors. Compare
memory systems by substrate, specificity, duration, and recall mechanism rather
than by whether a second response is simply "bigger."
