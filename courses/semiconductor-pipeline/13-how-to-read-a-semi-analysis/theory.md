## Mental models

**Constraint migration:** In one quarter, the limit may be HBM stacks. Two
quarters later, it may be advanced packaging. A year later, data-center power or
networking may be the governor. Analysts must update the bottleneck, not just
repeat the original story.

**Two clocks:** Product demand can move in weeks, but fabs, EUV tools, packaging
capacity, and power interconnects move in quarters or years. A company can be
simultaneously demand-rich and supply-constrained.

**Substitution ladder:** Ask what customers can do if supply is short. Can they
buy a competing chip, use a lower-bandwidth memory stack, redesign the package,
move workloads to another accelerator, rent cloud capacity, optimize software,
or wait? Pricing power is stronger when substitution is slow and painful.

**Qualification lock-in:** Semiconductor customers do not swap suppliers as
casually as software users switch apps. A qualified process node, HBM stack,
package flow, board design, rack design, tool recipe, or software stack can
create stickiness.

**Mix shift:** Revenue growth is not enough. The mix of products can raise or
lower margins. HBM can be margin-accretive for memory makers. Full data-center
systems can be more complex and cost-heavy than selling accelerator boards.

**Allocation power:** In shortages, the supplier who decides which customer gets
scarce product can capture economics even without raising list price. Allocation
power can show up as larger commitments, strategic partnerships, richer system
bundles, or better payment terms.

**Narrative half-life:** A true story can become stale. "Packaging is the
bottleneck" may be right for one product generation and incomplete for the next.
Always attach a date and a product generation to the claim.

## Source-aware caveats

Every source has a native bias:

| Source | Bias to remember | Practical caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Company management | Wants investors to see durable demand and controlled execution | Translate adjectives into measurable claims |
| Customers | May want supplier leverage or investor permission for capex | Separate procurement intent from deployed capacity |
| Suppliers | May describe end-market strength without naming the customer | Match product cycles before connecting the dots |
| Channel checks | Can reveal inflections early | Ask whether the sample is representative |
| Policy reporting | Can move quickly | Verify effective dates, license scope, and product applicability |
| Sell-side models | Useful for consensus shape | Treat precise numbers as estimates, not facts |

The point is not cynicism. It is calibration. A careful analyst can use biased
sources if the bias is visible.

## A semiconductor evidence ladder

Use this ladder when turning loose facts into confidence:

1. **Mechanism exists.** The product needs a scarce input, such as HBM,
   leading-edge wafers, advanced packaging, or power.
2. **Constraint is current.** Multiple sources indicate the input is limiting
   shipments now.
3. **Company has priority.** The company has allocation, qualification, or
   customer pull that lets it receive scarce supply.
4. **Economics are captured.** Revenue, margin, commitments, or customer terms
   show the company benefits rather than merely passes through cost.
5. **Constraint persists long enough.** The bottleneck is not solved before the
   company can monetize it.

Many weak theses stop at step 1. SemiAnalysis-style thinking pushes toward step
5 and keeps asking what changes the answer.

## The "so what?" test

Every fact should answer one of three questions:

- Does this increase or reduce scarcity?
- Does this change who captures economics?
- Does this change the timing of supply, demand, or substitution?

If a fact does none of those, it may be interesting but not thesis-critical.
