## From electron density to molecular behavior

A structural formula hides a lot of physics behind short line segments. The
core idea is that electron density determines both **what a molecule can
interact with** and **where those interactions can occur**.

For a bond between atoms A and B, polarity is often summarized as:

$$
\Delta \chi = |\chi_A - \chi_B|
$$

where $\chi$ is electronegativity. Larger differences mean more polarized
bonds, though the surrounding molecule also matters.

## Dipoles add as vectors

A polar bond has a bond dipole, but a molecule's net polarity depends on
geometry. Carbon dioxide has polar C=O bonds, but the molecule is linear:

```text
O=C=O
```

The two bond dipoles point in opposite directions and largely cancel. Water has
two polar O-H bonds in a bent shape, so the dipoles do not cancel. This is why
geometry is part of chemistry, not decoration.

## Resonance and partial bond order

In resonance-stabilized systems, bond order may be fractional. A carboxylate
group is commonly drawn with one C=O and one C-O^- bond, but the two oxygens
are equivalent in the resonance hybrid:

$$
\mathrm{R{-}COO^-}
$$

The negative charge is shared over both oxygens. Spreading charge over more
atoms usually lowers energy, especially when the atoms are electronegative.

For amides, the important resonance pattern is:

$$
\mathrm{O{=}C{-}N \leftrightarrow O^-{-}C{=}N^+}
$$

This gives the C-N bond partial double-bond character. In proteins, every
peptide bond is an amide, so this local planarity is repeated along the whole
backbone.

## Sterics: atoms take up space

Atoms are not points. When groups are forced too close together, electron
clouds repel; this is **steric strain**. Sterics can make one conformation much
more likely than another even when the bonds are formally allowed to rotate.

This matters in proteins because side chains compete for space. A favorable
hydrogen bond does not help if the atoms cannot reach the right geometry
without crashing into the rest of the molecule.

## Geometry checklist

When you see a new biological molecule, ask:

1. Which atoms are electronegative?
2. Which bonds are polar?
3. Which atoms carry formal charge or likely partial charge?
4. Which groups have resonance?
5. Which atoms are sp, sp2, or sp3?
6. Which parts are rigid, planar, or freely rotatable?

That checklist turns a flat structure into a first draft of chemical behavior.
