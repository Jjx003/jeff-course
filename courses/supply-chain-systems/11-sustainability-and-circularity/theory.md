# Scope and Traceability

Greenhouse-gas accounting commonly separates:

- scope 1: direct emissions from owned or controlled operations
- scope 2: purchased electricity, steam, heating, and cooling
- scope 3: upstream and downstream value-chain emissions

For many firms, scope 3 dominates. That means supplier data quality, product design, and logistics choices matter.

## Functional units

Compare environmental impact using functional units. Examples:

- kg CO2e per delivered case
- kg CO2e per usable machine-hour
- liters of water per kilogram of finished product
- percent recycled content per compliant unit

Without a functional unit, teams can improve a metric by shifting volume or outsourcing the footprint.

## Circularity

Circular supply chains design for repair, reuse, remanufacturing, recycling, and recovery.

Circularity requires reverse logistics and quality sorting. A returned item is not automatically reusable. The system must decide whether to inspect, refurbish, harvest parts, recycle, or dispose.

## Tradeoffs

Sustainability decisions can conflict with cost, resilience, and service. Air freight may protect customer service but raise emissions. Local sourcing may reduce transport emissions but increase production energy use. Reusable packaging may require reverse flows and cleaning capacity.

A rigorous approach quantifies tradeoffs and makes the value judgment explicit.
