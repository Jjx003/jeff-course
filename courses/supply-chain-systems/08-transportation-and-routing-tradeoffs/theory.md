# Mode Choice

Transportation modes trade cost, speed, reliability, capacity, flexibility, and emissions.

| Mode | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| ocean | low cost per unit | slow and disruption-prone at ports |
| rail | efficient over land | less flexible routing |
| truck | flexible and fast regionally | driver, fuel, and congestion exposure |
| air | fastest | expensive and emissions-intensive |
| parcel | high service granularity | costly for bulky or low-margin goods |

## Consolidation

Consolidation lowers transportation cost per unit but can increase waiting time and inventory. The economic question is whether saved freight cost exceeds extra holding cost, delay cost, and service risk.

## Service promises

A delivery promise is a capacity commitment. If marketing promises two-day delivery everywhere, operations must hold more inventory near demand, use faster modes, or accept more expediting.

## Routing constraints

Real routing is constrained by vehicle capacity, driver hours, appointment windows, hazardous-material rules, temperature control, and delivery sequencing.

Even simple route changes can shift cost between teams. A full-truckload plan may minimize transport cost while forcing stores to receive awkward quantities or warehouses to hold too much inventory.

## Emissions

Supply-chain emissions analysis distinguishes scope 1, scope 2, and scope 3. Transportation often lives in scope 3 for shippers using carriers. A rigorous decision compares emissions per unit delivered, not only total emissions, and includes service and inventory consequences.
