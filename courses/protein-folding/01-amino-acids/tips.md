## Going deeper

- **Wikipedia: Amino acid** — [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acid). Solid reference with chemical structure diagrams for all 20.
- **Wikipedia: Proteinogenic amino acid** — [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proteinogenic_amino_acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proteinogenic_amino_acid). Covers the 20 standard plus selenocysteine and pyrrolysine.
- **Philip Ball, _How Life Works_** (2024). A genuinely accessible book-length tour of how proteins, DNA, and cells fit together — recommended by Magnus Ross as the single best lay-reader entry point into molecular biology.
- **PDB-101 "What is a protein?"** — [https://pdb101.rcsb.org/learn/guide-to-understanding-pdb-data/introduction](https://pdb101.rcsb.org/learn/guide-to-understanding-pdb-data/introduction). The Protein Data Bank's free educational site, full of structural figures and example proteins.

## Common pitfalls

- **Don't confuse "amino acid" with "residue".** They're the same thing
  *chemically*, but "amino acid" usually means a free molecule in solution
  and "residue" means one in its place inside a protein chain. Papers will
  say "residue 47" and "amino acid `K`" — the convention matters in context.
- **The one-letter codes are not mnemonic.** `K` is lysine because all the
  obvious letters (`L`, `Y`) were already taken. Look the table up; don't
  trust your intuition.
- **Cysteine is a wildcard.** Two cysteines in the same chain may or may not
  form a disulfide bond, and whether they do depends on the local
  environment (oxidising vs reducing). If you mutate a cysteine, you may be
  breaking a structurally critical bridge — or no bridge at all. There is
  no way to tell from the letter alone.
